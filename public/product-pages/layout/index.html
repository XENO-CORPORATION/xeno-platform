The water keeps its own calendar

The first thing you learn on the estuary is that the water keeps its own calendar. Twice a day it leaves, and twice a day it comes back a little later than it did the day before, so that a week of mornings walks the whole tide around the clock.

Mara Okafor has fished this stretch for nineteen years. She keeps the tables pinned inside the wheelhouse, but she rarely reads them now. The birds tell her first, she says: the oystercatchers lift off the mud a quarter of an hour before the channel starts to fill.

In the village the tide shapes everything that is not bolted down. The café opens at low water, when the walkers come across the flats, and closes when the causeway goes under. The school bus has two timetables. The post arrives by road or by boat depending on the month.

None of this feels like hardship to the people who live here. It feels like attention. You cannot plan a week without looking at the water, and after a while you stop wanting to. The days arrange themselves around a rhythm older than any of the buildings.

The harbourmaster keeps a ledger of every spring tide since 1952, written in four different hands. The highest mark is from a February night when the sea came up the high street and stood a foot deep outside the chapel. Nobody talks about it much. The line is painted on the wall, and that is enough.

By late afternoon the light goes flat and silver and the far bank disappears. The boats swing on their moorings to face the incoming water, all at once, like a crowd turning towards a sound. Then the channel fills, the mud becomes a mirror, and for an hour the whole estuary holds still.

Mara ties up at the quay as the last of the ebb runs out. Tomorrow she will leave fifty minutes later than today. She already knows what the birds will do.
